Executive Summary

What this atlas is, what it proposes, and how to use it.

What is the Dream Federation?

A proposed cooperative federation of 100 independent creative co-ops and 11 collectively owned corporations, designed to compete at major-studio scale while preserving local autonomy, worker ownership, and democratic governance.

Think of it as a studio that is also a network of studios — each with its own identity, specialism, and members, all bound by a common plan.

What problem does it solve?

Creative studios face a tension: scale requires coordination, but coordination can stifle the independence and identity that makes the work valuable in the first place.

The federation model resolves this by separating ownership from control: local co-ops own their assets and IP, while the federation provides binding direction for shared projects and infrastructure.

How is this different?

No founder veto. Decisions are made democratically, one co-op one vote.

Private IP stays private. Founder-owned work (P00) remains outside the collective group.

Workers earn more. The model ensures actual workers benefit from the value they create.

Phase 1 · Foundation — years 1–3
Three real operating co-ops, twelve months of accounts, signed service arrangements and functioning elections. F00 incorporates only then; H00 and corporations remain options, not obligations.

Ten or more co-ops; recurring originals with funded completion plans; monthly 13-week cash forecasts; the first trading corporations (C01 distribution, C03 publishing) open to outside customers as well as members.

Members set the constitution

One ordinary vote per admitted co-op. The assembly elects the board and adopts the powers, obligations and reserved decisions. The founder has no permanent veto, no founder-owned percentage of federation value and no investor seat at F00.

The board commits the slate

A mandatory annual capacity compact identifies each member's funded capacity, delivery obligations, charges and risk limits. Binding standards apply throughout membership. A dissenting vote is not an opt-out.

The executive directs delivery

A programme executive assigns and sequences work through local delivery officers and authorised corporate managers. A valid within-scope instruction does not require another local vote.

Local authority is preserved

Member co-ops choose their management, negotiate service prices and accept or reject new work. The federation can set conditions for its finance, shared brand and distribution services, but it should not quietly turn those conditions into unrestricted employment control.

One vote, no founder veto

The initial voting proposal is one admitted co-op, one ordinary vote. BC's Cooperative Association Act provides a specific equitable-voting route for corporate bodies organized on a cooperative basis; any later weighting must be designed within that framework. There is no permanent founder veto and no founder-owned percentage of federation value.

Worker Congress and councils

Elected delegates from co-ops and corporate workforces, including a route for non-member and project workers, review labour outcomes, propose charter changes and trigger investigations. Sector and Creative Councils coordinate pipelines and standards; neither can appropriate another enterprise's IP.

Boundaries on binding authority

Binding authority needs clear boundaries: a budget, available rights, a named recipient, a deadline and an accountable decision-maker.

  • Local members retain their enterprise and protected workplace powers.
  • The executive cannot appropriate private IP, waive earned pay or order unlawful or unsafe work.
  • Material scope changes require authorised funding and a recorded variation.
  • An operational appeal does not automatically suspend a valid instruction; legal and safety protections remain available.
  • Substantiated illegality, safety, rights or out-of-scope funding grounds may suspend only the affected requirement, with prompt review.

Decision authority and safeguards

  • Local hiring, pay and working methods stay with the local employer and member governance.
  • A project using only local resources is approved by the local co-op board or authorized team.
  • Use of C09 finance or shared guarantees goes through the credit committee and relevant boards.
  • Sale of major collective assets or structural conversion requires the statutory and constitutional bodies, with creditor and class rights preserved.
  • Founder compensation and IP transactions go to an independent committee with founder recusal and combined related-party disclosure.

The initial CAD 100 million budget is funded separately: 30 in fixed territory pre-sales, 15 in eligible production credits, 35 in equity and 20 in senior borrowing. Changing senior principal or equity here changes the recoupment claim only. It does not produce a revised, balanced production-financing plan. The initial budget and its financing are a fixed reference; changes require a separate sources-and-uses review.

The distribution fee and IP royalty are calculated on the same defined later receipts. Fixed residuals come next, followed by debt principal, return of equity, the fixed preferred return, and the assumed tax/reserve provision. The remaining cash is split between investor back end and the producing co-op. The co-op remainder is then allocated to workers, local capital and federation development.

The preferred percentage is a one-time return on original equity, not an annual interest rate. Unpaid principal, preferred return and other claimed amounts remain visible. Unrecovered equity and a cash-reserve shortfall are not automatically debts; their legal status follows the actual agreement. Actual taxes, secured-creditor rights and labour obligations can change the sequence. This ordering is an example, not a legal payment-priority opinion.

The tax/reserve provision is a chosen cash requirement, not a computed tax liability. It stays fixed even in weak outcomes to expose unmet provision; change it only with a reason. FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about the federation model, governance, financing, and implementation.

What is a cooperative federation?

A cooperative federation is a higher-level cooperative whose members are themselves cooperatives. In this model, F00 (the federation) is owned and democratically controlled by its member co-ops, each of which has one vote regardless of size or contribution.

This is different from a traditional holding company or parent corporation, which typically has investor ownership and top-down control. In a federation, power flows upward from members, not downward from owners.

How is this different from a traditional studio?

Traditional studios are typically hierarchical: a central management makes decisions that cascade down to departments. In the federation model, each co-op retains local autonomy over its own work, hiring, and creative decisions.

The federation provides binding direction only for shared projects — when multiple co-ops are collaborating on a production that uses collective resources or infrastructure. For purely local work, the co-op's own governance applies.

What happens when co-ops disagree with federation decisions?

Disagreement is expected and built into the model. The federation's authority is not absolute:

  • Local autonomy is preserved: Co-ops can reject federation work and pursue independent projects elsewhere.
  • Democratic appeal: Decisions can be challenged through the assembly, which has the power to remove directors or the CEO.
  • Limited scope: The federation can only direct work that uses collective resources or affects the network as a whole.
  • Exit option: Co-ops can withdraw from the federation (subject to statutory rules and contractual obligations).
How does the founder maintain creative control?

The founder maintains creative control through private IP ownership, not through governance power. P00 (the founder's private IP company) remains 100% founder-owned and outside the collective group.

P00 licenses its IP to federation productions through arm's-length contracts. The federation cannot appropriate or control P00's IP. However, when P00 work is produced using federation resources, it must follow the same rules as any other production.

How are workers compensated?

Workers are compensated through a five-tier payment system that ensures clarity and fairness:

  1. Contractual wages: Paid first, never waived to fund pools or reserves.
  2. Creative participation: Based on contract terms and defined receipts.
  3. Residual participation: Royalties and backend payments per project terms.
  4. Discretionary surplus: Distributed after obligations, taxes, reserves, and solvency tests.
  5. Federation patronage: Paid to member co-ops by eligible businesses, then applied by local policy.

Key principle: These payments are never blended — each has distinct rules, timing, and accounting.

What prevents the federation from becoming bureaucratic?

Several mechanisms prevent bureaucracy:

  • Enumerated powers: The federation can only act in areas explicitly delegated to it.
  • Local first: Decisions are made at the lowest possible level (co-op, then sector, then federation).
  • Evidence-based: Major decisions require documented evidence and independent review.
  • Sunset clauses: Powers and structures have defined review periods and expiration dates.
  • Accountability: Directors and the CEO can be removed by the assembly at any time.
GL

Glossary

Key terms and definitions for understanding the federation model.

Assembly
The supreme decision-making body of F00, composed of one delegate from each member co-op. Has authority to approve constitutional amendments, elect/remove directors and CEO, and override federation decisions.
Collective Commercial Group (CCG)
The group of entities (co-ops and other businesses) that are members of F00 and contribute to its collective resources. Includes all sector clusters and P00.
Cooperative Federation (F00)
The central federation entity, a higher-level cooperative owned and democratically controlled by its member co-ops. Provides binding direction for shared projects and collective infrastructure.
Discretionary Surplus
The fifth tier of worker compensation. Distributed after all obligations, taxes, reserves, and solvency tests are met. Each payment type has distinct rules and accounting.
Enumerated Powers
The principle that F00 can only act in areas explicitly delegated to it by its constitution and member agreements. Prevents mission creep and bureaucratic expansion.
Federation Patronage
Payments made to member co-ops by eligible businesses (those meeting certain criteria). These funds are then applied according to each co-op's local policy.
Local Autonomy
The principle that each co-op retains control over its own work, hiring, and creative decisions. Federation direction only applies to shared projects using collective resources.
P00
The founder's private IP company, 100% founder-owned and outside the collective group. Licenses IP to federation productions through arm's-length contracts.
Sector Cluster
A grouping of co-ops within a specific industry sector (e.g., Animation, Games, Music). Each sector has its own governance and can elect representatives to federation bodies.
Sunset Clause
A provision that causes certain powers, structures, or agreements to automatically expire or require renewal after a defined period. Ensures regular review and prevents stagnation.
